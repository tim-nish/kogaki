## Trigger — what happened

kogaki#804 carried acceptance criterion 1 as a grep that must return nothing for a deleted carrier's name. At pickup the owner ruled separately that passages describing the removed behaviour go while citations grounding decisions that still stand are kept. One recorded git command in a design document contains the deleted path inside the command it ran; deleting it would falsify the record of what was run, and keeping it makes the grep return one line. The criterion and the ruling were both the owner's, four hours apart.

## The learning

When an issue carries both a mechanical acceptance criterion and a later human ruling, the criterion is the one that keeps being trusted, because it reads as objective and can be evaluated without judgment. A grep returning zero looks like proof; the ruling that narrows what should have been counted looks like commentary. The failure is not that the criterion is wrong — it was right when written — but that nothing marks it as scoped by a decision made afterwards. Two practices follow. State a criterion's population, not just its predicate: 'no live carrier or pointer names X' is checkable and survives a later ruling about history, while 'the grep returns nothing' does not. And when a ruling narrows a criterion, say so at the criterion, in the same act, rather than leaving the next reader to discover that the objective-looking half is the stale one.
