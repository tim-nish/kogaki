## Trigger — what happened

A report said an act was "correct when the whole item moved, wrong when only part did", and named two symptoms under that one condition. Running the act on a whole move — the case the report called correct — showed one symptom firing anyway. The two symptoms had different conditions, and the sentence joining them hid the fact that one of them fires every single time.

## The learning

When a report names several symptoms under one condition, check each symptom against the condition separately before accepting the grouping. A shared sentence is not evidence of a shared cause, and the grouping is usually written from the case where all the symptoms appeared together — which is the case that cannot distinguish them. The cost of the wrong grouping is not just an imprecise report: it makes the proposed repairs look like alternatives to choose between, when they actually address different symptoms and one of them may be needed regardless of which is chosen. Here the narrower-looking repair addressed the rarer symptom, and the symptom that fires unconditionally had no advocate at all, because the sentence implied it was already covered. The way to catch it is to run the act in the case the report calls fine. If a symptom fires there, its condition is not the one the report gave it.
