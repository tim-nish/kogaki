## Trigger — what happened

A plan to clear out a large pile of stale work items was approved, and by the time it ran the pile had already been worked through — 41 items down to 11, with 96 finished in the six days between the approval and the attempt.

## The learning

When a plan is approved to act on a group of things, the approval is tied to the group as it was counted at that moment, not to whatever the group happens to hold later. Work that is going to be carried out after some delay should therefore re-count the group first and stop if the count no longer matches, showing what changed. Quietly acting on the current contents instead is the dangerous move, because the action still appears to succeed — it just succeeds against the wrong set, and nothing in the outcome reveals the substitution. Two further things this made clear. First, a backlog can empty out simply because the ordinary work continued, which settles the plan rather than defeating it: the pile the plan existed to clear was not disposed of, it was finished. Second, the re-count was only possible because whoever wrote the plan had written down the count and how they arrived at it. Had the plan said only 'the stale items', there would have been nothing to compare against and no way to notice the mismatch at all.
