## Trigger — what happened

A change that deleted a reversed rule from its governing spec left the same rule standing, verbatim and operative, in the actor's own instruction file seventy lines below the new instruction — and in two more carriers the diff touched, plus one it did not. An actor following the surviving paragraph produces a record the new code refuses outright.

## The learning

When a rule is deleted rather than amended, the deletion has to be swept across every carrier that a reader might ACT on, and the sweep's unit is the carrier's readers rather than the file list of the change. The instruction file for the actor whose behaviour the rule governs is the most dangerous residue site and the easiest to miss, because a change naturally edits the section it is adding to and not the section seventy lines away that says the opposite; the two paragraphs then contradict each other inside one document, and an actor is free to follow either. Two sharper points. A carrier the diff did not touch can still be falsified by it: a declaration naming a mechanism the change removed is now false even though its bytes are unchanged, so 'unchanged' is a claim about bytes and never about meaning, and a change that reports it as evidence of narrow scope has reported the wrong thing. And when a superseded rule is quoted in the new text as provenance, that quotation is not residue — the test for residue is whether a reader could act on the statement, not whether the old words appear.
