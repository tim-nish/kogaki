## Trigger — what happened

A commit message stated what the change did NOT do — 'no check is added for the word', carrying an owner instruction — and the boundary-receipt guard, which reads changed text as well as diff paths, matched the check/CI boundary on that very word. The branch touched one prose file and no check, hook or registry entry; the match existed only because the message disclaimed the act.

## The learning

A guard that reads text for the NAME of an act fires on the sentence that disclaims the act as readily as on the act itself, so writing down what you deliberately did not do is a way to trigger it. The tempting repair is to reword until the match goes away, which is the one move that must not be taken: it removes the evidence rather than the condition, and it trains the writer to avoid the guard's vocabulary instead of consulting. Where the guard binds on a receipt's existence rather than on the consultation's correctness, the incidental match costs exactly one consultation and the receipt is the discharge — the cheap bounded act the blocked party can always perform. Read the polarity before reaching for the editor: a guard whose discharge is cheap and available to the blocked party is designed to tolerate false matches, and dodging it is strictly worse than paying it.
