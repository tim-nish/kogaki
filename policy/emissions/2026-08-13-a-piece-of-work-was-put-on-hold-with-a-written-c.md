## Trigger — what happened

A piece of work was put on hold with a written condition for resuming it, plus a specific procedure for checking whether that condition had been met. The condition was eventually checked by running the procedure, and it had been met. But the case that met it turned out to be one the proposed fix would not have handled — the fix targeted a narrower shape than the case that actually arrived.

## The learning

A hold's resume condition and its proposed remedy are written at the same moment, from the same guess about what the problem will look like when it next appears. So they can both be reasonable and still disagree, and the disagreement is only visible at the moment the hold resumes — which is exactly when the pressure is to proceed with the plan already written. Checking the resuming case against the proposed fix, before implementing anything, is a cheap step that is easy to skip because the hold's own text reads like a decision already made. When they disagree, the resuming case is the better evidence: it is real and the remedy was hypothetical. What follows is that the remedy question reopens rather than the hold simply ending — and reopening it is not backsliding, because the alternative is shipping a fix whose own triggering evidence says it misses. Worth separating from the neighbouring case where a hold's condition never fires at all: this is the opposite failure, a condition that fires correctly and reveals the plan behind it was aimed elsewhere.
