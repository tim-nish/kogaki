## Trigger — what happened

A rule was reversed on a specimen that violated nothing: a 40-member group served flat and judged-empty was fully conformant, because the only cap bound a judgment that DID split and the standing rule made a no-split outcome legal at any size. The contract permitted the outcome, so the contract changed rather than the run being called a defect.

## The learning

When a bad outcome turns out to be CONFORMANT, that is a finding about the contract and not about the actor, and the reflex to look for who broke a rule wastes the specimen. The diagnostic move is to ask which rule would have had to exist for the outcome to be illegal, and then to notice why it did not: here the caps were all conditional on a decision the actor was free not to make, so every cap was live and none could reach the case. A prohibition that only binds inside a branch is invisible whenever the branch is not taken. Two riders travel with the repair. First, reversing a rule means deleting it from the operative carriers rather than marking it superseded, because a superseded contract kept as a record is a rule a later reader can still act on. Second, when a decision moves from an actor's judgment to the engine, check every existing fallback that let the actor decline — one of them will be the exact outcome the new rule refuses, and the two will contradict for precisely the population the new rule was written for, so the fallback must be bounded in the same act or the system produces a state it then refuses, with no legal rendering at all.
