## Trigger — what happened

A document was amended to promise something the code did not yet do. The paragraph defending that gap said the follow-up work was 'named rather than assumed' - and named nothing, because the follow-up item was created ten minutes after the paragraph was committed. The sentence read as careful. It was the only thing standing between the change and the exact defect it cited a previous change for, and it pointed at nothing a reader could open.

## The learning

A promise with a named carrier and a promise with an unnamed one look almost identical in prose, and the difference is the whole of whether the promise is honest. What makes this easy to get wrong is ordering rather than intent: the sentence is written while the thing it points at is still a plan, the plan gets executed minutes later, and nobody re-reads the sentence because it was true in spirit the whole time. Two habits close it. Create the thing first and paste its real identifier, even when that means an extra step in an order that feels backwards; and treat every reference you wrote as a promise until you have followed it yourself from the committed text, because a reference you only intended to make is indistinguishable, to everyone downstream, from one you made and got wrong. Where the identifier genuinely cannot exist yet, say that plainly - 'the carrier is owed and unfiled' is a weaker claim than a number, and a weaker true claim beats a stronger unfollowable one.
