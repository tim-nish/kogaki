id: make_a_three_level_distinction_concrete_with_a_familiar_chain
before: >-
  knowledge: three related terms may feel interchangeable. question: holds
  an unanswered question about how the terms differ in practice.
  orientation: treats the terms as neighboring labels rather than a
  structured hierarchy.
after: >-
  knowledge: each term occupies a distinct level, identified by the function
  it performs for the level above it. question: the difference is settled by
  a complete example that assigns all three terms consistently. orientation:
  the terms form a directional hierarchy in which each lower level serves
  the one above it.
question: >-
  holds: how do the three terms differ in practice? settles: how do the
  three terms differ in practice?
order: >-
  raises, advances, advances, advances, advances, settles. The sequence
  first stabilizes definitions, then compresses them into a memorable verbal
  form, then supplies a familiar case, and finally reconstructs that case as
  a directional chain, so the reader receives a criterion before being asked
  to apply it.
presupposes: >-
  a case exists that is familiar enough to the reader that its parts can be
  recognized without further argument.
technique: >-
  Define each level of a hierarchy by the function it performs for the level
  immediately above it, in ascending order. Compress the set of definitions
  into one parallel question per level so the distinction becomes memorable
  rather than merely stated. Then select one sequence from a domain the
  reader already knows, assign each of its parts to a level, and restate the
  sequence in causal order so every assignment can be checked against the
  reader's own sense of how that sequence runs.
breaks: >-
  remove: dropping the familiar sequence leaves only abstract definitions,
  and the reader has no case against which to test whether the levels can be
  told apart. reorder: introducing the sequence before the definitions hands
  the reader labeled parts with no criterion yet for what distinguishes the
  labels, so the mapping cannot be verified. extend: pressing further to
  judge whether the sequence's particular instance actually succeeds shifts
  the reader from learning the hierarchy to arguing about the case's truth,
  which the technique does not support.
draws_on: >-
  the reader's own world — a sequence assumed already familiar supplies the
  concrete instance the abstract hierarchy is mapped onto and checked
  against.
