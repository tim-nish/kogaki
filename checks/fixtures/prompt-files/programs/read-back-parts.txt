# Read back the plan of one paragraph

## The plan of this paragraph

### The structure the paragraph conveys

The table holds every thing this paragraph speaks of and how those things are joined.
A symbol in braces stands for the row that carries it.
A Role is a thing, and its sentence says what the thing is.
A Relation joins the rows in its Arguments column, and its sentence says how.

| symbol | name | kind | Arguments | sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| {r_1} | installer | Role |  | the program that copies the kit. |
| {r_2} | copy | Role |  | the files it writes. |
| {c_1} | writes | Relation | {r_1}, {r_2} | The {r_1} writes the {r_2}. |

The CentralClaim of this paragraph is {c_1}.
Its Arguments are {r_1}, {r_2}.

### How the paragraph uses the structure

The paragraph gives the reader the CentralClaim.
The CentralClaim is how its Arguments stand to each other, and the reader does not have that yet.

The reader already knows every Argument.
Give an Argument only the words the reader needs to tell which known thing is meant: the name of a Role in it, a few words of it, or a word that points back to it.
A reader told at length what they already know reads less closely, and then misses the CentralClaim.
The paragraph's words go to the CentralClaim, because it is the one thing the reader does not have.

Write the paragraph in words you choose for this reader.
The sentences in the table are written to fix the structure exactly, and a reader hears them as a plan.
A name in the table labels its row for the program that built the table.
In the paragraph each thing gets the word this reader would use for it.
One thing keeps one name through the whole paragraph.
A reader takes a second name for a second thing.

### The parts of the paragraph, in order

Each numbered line below is one part.
It begins with the symbol of the row the part states, or with Neither where it states no row.
A part is one sentence.
A part about an Argument may instead be a few words inside the sentence of the part next to it.
A part about an Argument brings it back that briefly.
The part about the CentralClaim says how its Arguments stand to each other, and brings each Argument back as briefly as any other part does.
A part marked Neither works only with what the table holds.
The table holds everything this paragraph tells the reader.
A line may say how its part stands to an earlier part.
It names that earlier part before the colon.
The text after the colon speaks of two sentences only, this part and the part named before the colon.
In that text, "this sentence" is this part.
In that text, "the sentence pointed to" is the part named before the colon.
The text ends by saying what the two sentences do for the reader.
Where a line names several earlier parts, those parts together are the sentence pointed to.
Where a line holds several texts, the part is one sentence that fits all of them.
A part that later parts point to also does what each of their lines says of the sentence pointed to.

1. {r_1}. Pointed to by part 2.
2. Neither. To part 1: the sentence pointed to raises a question, and this sentence gives its answer. The reader is given the question first, so the answer arrives as something they were waiting for.

## How the prose reads

**Language.** Write this Leg in English.

**The reader's own world.** The reader has installed a program from a package manager.

**What the register below governs.** The register governs how sentences read.
What the paragraph says comes from the plan above.
Where a register rule and the plan conflict, the plan holds and the rule yields for that sentence.
Write that sentence as the plan has it and go on to the next.

**Sentences.** Classic register, in full clauses, each with a concrete subject that acts.
A term of art is explained where it first appears.
Sentences that belong together are joined into one, so that a short sentence stands among longer ones.
A list holds as many items as the content has.
A sentence says what a thing is and what it does.

**Paragraphs.** A paragraph's first sentence names in full each thing it refers to.
Inside a paragraph, reference is free.

**Referents.** A referent is a case, example, file or person introduced in the Journey block, and a later paragraph may name it again by its name.
Every other thing the prose names, a thing from the reader's own world included, lives inside its own paragraph.
A comparison explains a term once, in the place where it stands.

## Read back

Everything above is what a writer is handed for one paragraph.
This call does not write the paragraph.
For each part of the plan you answer two things.

The first is the readback.
The readback says, in your own words, what the plan tells the writer to do in that part.
It says what the part must say.
It says how the part must stand to each earlier part it points to.
Where the plan tells the writer nothing for a part, the readback is null.

The second is the sample.
The sample is the one sentence you would write for that part.
It follows your readback and nothing else.
Where the readback is null, the sample is null.

Answer as exactly this JSON record and nothing else:
{ "parts": [ { "part": <number>, "readback": <text or null>, "sample": <one sentence or null> } ] }
The record holds one entry for each part, in order.
